Lee y explora la India

A walk-up, bilingual wayfinding kiosk for India’s Guest of Honour Pavilion at FILBo 2026, the Bogotá International Book Fair. Designed in Figma and coded to run live on a 43-inch touch screen.

Bogotá, Colombia

21 April – 4 May 2026

The final home screen, live on the kiosk: tap a zone, see the walking path from “You are here”.

Kiosk Interface

Overview

India was the Guest of Honour Country at FILBo 2026. Over 600,000 visitors walked through the fair, and the India Pavilion was one of its largest stops.

The pavilion was an irregular, triangle-shaped floor with a dozen zones: exhibitions, a children’s forest, a publishers’ street, a bookstore, a stage and a café. Most visitors spoke Spanish. Most of the signage was in English and Hindi. I designed and built the touch kiosk that sat at the entry lobby and helped people find their way, in their own language, in a few seconds.

The context

A pavilion that reads like a journey

India was the Guest of Honour Country at the 2026 Bogotá International Book Fair. The Ministry of Education organised the pavilion with the National Book Trust as implementing agency, and NID designed it around one idea: the Indian kite, stories in flight across languages and borders.

The brief

Inside the pavilion were seven exhibitions, a collective exhibit of Indian books, a children’s zone, a publishers’ street, a bookstore, a stage for talks and performances, and a café. My job was the digital layer on top of the physical one: a kiosk at the entry lobby that could orient a first-time visitor and send them to the right zone.

The mascot

A family reading together, drawn in the bold outlines of Kalighat folk art. It sits in the corner of every kiosk screen as a friendly anchor and reminds visitors that reading in India is shared across generations.

03

The problem

Find your way in seconds, in a language you speak

A visitor at the entry lobby has three questions and very little patience. The kiosk had to answer all three before the next person stepped up.

Q1

Where am I?

A clear, fixed “You are here” at the entry lobby, oriented the way the visitor is standing.

Q2

Where is it?

Every zone reachable in one tap, with a walking path drawn from the visitor’s spot to the door of the zone.

Q3

What’s there?

A short, bilingual description and a picture of the zone, so people decide before they walk.

Constraints on the floor

Walk-up, zero onboarding

No instructions, no login. The first tap had to be obvious from three metres away.

Spanish-first audience

Content in English and Spanish, switchable from any screen, with Hindi kept for zone titles and identity.

43-inch landscape, standing height

Large touch targets in the lower two-thirds of the screen; nothing important hidden in corners.

Crowds and idle time

Back to an attract loop on its own when no one is using it, so the next visitor always starts fresh.

Must not fail live

Fourteen days of continuous running on site, so the build had to be simple, local and robust.

How might we

Turn a crowded, twelve-zone pavilion into one calm screen that tells anyone, in English or Spanish, where they are, where to go and why it’s worth the walk?

The build

From Figma frames to a running kiosk

I didn’t hand the screens off. I vibe-coded them into a small, self-contained web app: plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript, no framework, opened full-screen on the 43-inch kiosk. What visitors touched was exactly what was designed.

4k

lines of HTML, CSS and JavaScript

6

screen states in one page

12

zones driven from one data file

100 s

until the kiosk resets itself

How the code is split

data.js

Zones, EN / ES copy, map labels, shapes, help points

537 lines

→

state.js

UI state, translation helpers, scale-to-fit

48 lines

→

ui.js

Rendering, taps, footsteps, music, idle timer

1,732 lines

←

footsteps-data.js

Generated path data for every route

generated

Scales to any screen

The app is laid out on a fixed 1920 × 1080 stage and scaled to fit whatever display it opens on.

Screens as states

attract → welcome → namaste → explore → kiosk_loading → map, all one page driven by a single state object.

Bilingual by data

Every string lives in English and Spanish dictionaries, with English as the fallback, so the toggle swaps text in place.

Footsteps that replay

A run counter forces a clean replay when the same zone is tapped twice, so the route always animates.

Idle reset

After 100 seconds without a touch the kiosk drops back into the attract loop for the next visitor.

Kiosk-proof

Version tags on every asset beat aggressive kiosk caching, and a validate script checks syntax and missing asset files.

Reflections

What a kiosk taught me about maps

Designing for a screen people meet once, standing up, in a crowd, changed how I think about information design.

01

The map is the menu

Making the floor plan the home screen removed a whole layer of navigation. People understood it without reading.

02

Borrow, don’t invent

Reusing the pavilion’s motifs and colours meant the screen taught the signage, and the signage confirmed the screen.

06

The Motif & Identity Explorations

A visual Identity borrowed from different Indian Art Style

Each zone of the pavilion draws on a different Indian art tradition: Spiti’s woven textiles, Hampi’s stone streets, Lucknow’s chikankari and Kalighat’s bold folk outlines. Each tradition became a motif, a colour pair and an icon. The kiosk carried these onto the screen, so the hanging signs, the printed panels and the map tiles all share one visual language.
