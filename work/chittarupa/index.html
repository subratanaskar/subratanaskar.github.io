How much do we know about the Goddess Durga?

Introduction

Every autumn, Bengal stops to welcome a daughter home. Durga Puja is the largest festival of West Bengal, but for many young Bengalis it has become a week of pandals, lights and food, while the stories, rituals and meanings inside it quietly slip away.

Chittarupa (চিত্তরূপা, the form of consciousness) is a design interpretation of Durga. It reads her journey, from Ganga clay to the river again, as a mirror of the human life cycle, and keeps the heritage in daily use through a story told in Kalighat pat, ritual explainers, a Mahalaya Radio and a map of Bengal's pandals.

Durga Puja is a ten-day worship of the mother goddess Durga, celebrated across West Bengal each September or October. Its public form grew from rajbari courtyards (Sovabazar, 1757) to the first barowari puja in Guptipara (1761 or 1790) and Kolkata's first community puja in Bhowanipore (1909). Today thousands of neighbourhood committees build pandals, Kumartuli's artisans sculpt idols from Ganga clay, and the city becomes one of the world's largest public art festivals.

Tuku: “Ma, why does Durga come every year?”

Ma: “Because she is Uma, a daughter coming home to her parents. Once a year, with all four children.”

Ma: “Every daughter returns to her own home. She goes back to Kailash, to Shiva. So we say, she will come again next year.”

Tuku: “Then why does she go back after just a few days?”

Tuku: “And why does she have ten hands?”

Ma: “When a demon defeated the gods, they joined all their power. She was born from it, and each god gave her a weapon.”

Ma: “Ten hands for ten directions, to protect us from every side. Like a mother doing ten things at once.”

Tuku: “Like you, Ma!”

Ma: “She is a mother too, Tuku. That’s why she always comes back.”

Objective

Choose one place, craft, community or tradition, understand the people it belongs to, and design a way for it to stay useful and meaningful in everyday life, so heritage stays alive as it changes instead of being frozen behind glass.

How might we keep India's heritage alive while allowing communities, places and traditions to evolve?

I chose Durga Puja in West Bengal. It is not at risk of disappearing; millions celebrate it every year. What is fading is the meaning carried inside it: why the rituals happen, what the weapons stand for, how the story goes. Many young Bengalis know the pandals but not the story.

Who is being left out of this heritage?

Young people outside Bengal or away from family pujas, non-Bengali visitors, and anyone who cannot follow Purans and the traditions. Chittarupa opens the layer of meaning in plain English.

Who holds the knowledge, and how can it be shared?

Purohits, scholars of the Devi Mahatmya, Kumartuli artisans and dhakis. The site turns what they hold into stories, ritual explainers, a radio and a map.

Bengal's pujo

For a Bengali, the year is counted toward pujo. New clothes for each day, the Puja annuals, the 4 a.m. radio on Mahalaya, nights of pandal hopping, bhog khichuri, adda past midnight, and the quiet after Dashami. Across class, caste and religion, the city walks through the same streets together. It is less a festival than a season of homecoming.

What is fading

The festival itself is growing. What is thinning is the layer of meaning inside it. The rites are performed in Sanskrit, at speed, in a crowd. Young Bengalis in and outside Bengal know the pandals and the food, but often not the story of Mahakali and Mahishasura, why Kola Bou stands beside Ganesh, or why Sandhi Puja must happen in 48 minutes.

Is the crowd still celebrating the goddess, or only the spectacle around her?

THE PARA PANDAL

Every para (neighbourhood) builds its own pandal. Committees raise money for months, artists design themes, and the street becomes a temple, a gallery and an adda for five days. North Kolkata keeps the traditional ek-chala idols of Bagbazar and Kumartuli; south Kolkata is known for theme pandals that draw queues through the night.

The Bengali difference

Everywhere else she is mainly a warrior to be worshipped . In Bengal she is also a daughter who comes home — which is why her leaving on Dashami makes a whole city cry.

Where Chittarupa steps in

Four challenges sit in the top-right: language, fading knowledge, spectacle and distance . These are problems of explanation and access — exactly what an information designer can solve.

Project Purpose

Why???

People take part in pujo with devotion, excitement or habit , but rarely with understanding. Knowing why each ritual exists turns a crowd into participants who feel connected .

What???

Chittarupa gives structured, layered stories — myth, symbol and meaning — that help people move from watching to understanding .

How???

Through scrollytelling in Kalighat pat art , ritual cards read in four layers, a Mahalaya radio and a pandal map that works from anywhere .

For whom???

For the child who asks why , the Bengali who lives far away, and anyone who is curious but lost .

Key insights

We follow, we don’t ask

Most people perform rituals because elders do — not because they know why.

Celebration has replaced worship

Many go “because everyone is going”; the pandal matters more than the puja.

The deepest layer is empty

No one linked Durga’s journey to the human life cycle — the richest meaning is unknown.

Knowledge is locked with elders

Purohits explain only when asked, and few young people ask.

Distance creates longing

Those away from Bengal rely on YouTube, Mahalaya at dawn and friends’ videos.

Stories work better than facts

Opportunity

Design Brief

Design an interactive, story-led website that helps young Bengalis — and anyone curious — understand the meaning behind Maa Durga’s form, her weapons and the rituals of pujo, told in the visual language of Bengal itself.

How might we turn rituals people already perform into stories they understand?

How might we make the symbolism of ten weapons feel personal, not textbook?

How might we let someone far from Kolkata still feel pujo around them?

Alpona — the rice-paste floor art

Alpona is drawn with a finger dipped in rice paste, usually by women of the house, to welcome Lakshmi and Durga. I use it for borders, section dividers and loading animations — always white on clay-red, always hand-drawn in feel.

Paint style — Kalighat pat

Bold, sweeping outline — One confident brush stroke, no sketchy lines

Flat colour, soft shading — Shading only along the edges of limbs

Round faces, large eyes — Almond eyes and arched brows

Empty background — The figure is the whole story

Sindoor line art — Weapons redrawn in one solid #5E0202 line

The Website

Every screen, what it holds, and why it exists.

01

Home

Information — Hero title and tagline; Part 1 — Mahakali, the primordial void, in six scroll scenes; Part 2 — Mahishasura rises, the gods unite and Durga is born; explore cards to every chapter.

Interaction — Scroll-triggered scenes, parallax illustration, text that reveals line by line.

Purpose — Gives every visitor the core myth before anything else.

02

Journey — From Clay to Goddess

Information — Eight stages from Ganga mati to worship in the pandal, plus the story of nishiddho mrittika — clay from a sex worker’s doorstep.

Interaction — A vertical timeline; each stage opens with an image and a short caption.

Purpose — Answers “how is she made?” and shows the human hands behind her.

03

Weapons

Information — All ten weapons in Sindoor line art; for each, the god who gave it, what it represents and the inner quality it asks of us.

Interaction — Hover or click a weapon to reveal its story.

Purpose — Fills the biggest gap from interviews — only 5% knew the weapons.

04

Rituals — Six Days of Devotion

Information — The six days, Mahalaya to Dashami, as one row of cards.

Interaction — Click a day to open its page.

Purpose — Shows the order of the festival at a glance.

05

Ritual day — Ashtami

Information — Pushpanjali, Sandhi Puja and Dhunuchi Naach, each read in four layers: what happens, mythological reason, symbolic meaning, psychological & community effect.

Interaction — Layer-by-layer reveal; next and previous day.

Purpose — Explains the why — the core of the brief.

06

Reflections — The Mirror of All Lives

Information — Philosophical grounding, Charaka Samhita and the five elements, and a side-by-side mirror of Durga’s journey and a human life: birth, living, death.

Interaction — Parallels appear as you scroll.

Purpose — Turns the 0% gap — Durga and the human metaphor — into the emotional centre.

What did Chittarupa teach me?

— Research changed the project. I started with weapons and ended with people — Tuku’s questions and Arko’s distance shaped every page.

— Being Bengali was not enough. I had to unlearn what I thought I knew and listen to purohits, artisans and scholars.

— Style carries meaning. Moving from Madhubani to Kalighat pat made the work honest to the place it speaks about.

— Information design is storytelling. A list of facts became a journey once each fact answered a real question.
