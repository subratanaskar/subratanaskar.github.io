A tangible data visualisation that serves twelve everyday foods as kebab skewers on a live grill. Every block is a stage of the supply chain — and the smoke rising from it is the greenhouse gas we never see.

What sits on our plate is only the last step of a long, smoky journey.

Food accounts for about a quarter of global greenhouse gas emissions, but we usually only see the final dish, not the deforestation, farm methane, transport, packaging, or wasted food behind it.

​

The course asked us to turn a dataset into a physical form people could explore. I chose emissions from 1 kg of twelve common foods, detailed across their supply chains.

Designed for the everyday eater, not the climate scientist.

Primary audience

Everyday consumers making daily food choices — grocery shopping, deciding what to cook — without any background in climate science or supply-chain data.

Intent: make the invisible part of a meal visible in a few seconds, so a person can compare foods by feel, not by spreadsheet.

Digging into the data before touching a single rod.

I worked through Our World in Data’s writing on food and the environment. It rests on the largest meta-analysis of food systems to date — Poore & Nemecek (2018): 570 studies, ~38,700 farms in 119 countries, and 40 products covering about 90% of the world’s protein and calories.

stacks of OWID charts became my research wall

What the research told me

26%

of global greenhouse gas emissions come from the food system.

>80%

of most foods’ footprint comes from land use and the farm stage.

<1%

of beef’s emissions come from transport. Air freight is 0.16% of food miles.

1 day

a week swapping beef & dairy saves more than buying all food locally.

~2%

energy efficiency of cows — 100 kcal of feed returns ~2 kcal of beef.

¼

of food’s emissions come from food that is lost or wasted.

Nut orchards even show a negative land-use value — they can store more carbon than they emit.

Four questions, four surprising answers.

Q1

Does “buying local” actually reduce a food’s carbon footprint?

Barely. Transport is typically under 1% of beef’s footprint. What we eat matters far more than how far it travelled.

Q2

Are the highest-emission foods always meat and dairy?

No. Dark chocolate (46.6 kg) and coffee (28.5 kg) sit in my top four — pushed up by land-use change and losses.

Q3

Why do some animal foods have a far bigger footprint than others?

Efficiency. Smaller animals turn feed into food better: poultry is 9.9 kg CO₂e per kg, beef 99.5 kg.

Q4

Does eating less meat free up land, not just cut emissions?

Yes. Half of the world’s habitable land is farmed, and more plant-based diets need far less of it.

The big one: for the dirtiest foods, the culprit is land-use change and farm methane — not transport, retail or packaging.

From forty foods to twelve skewers.

The OWID dataset lists emissions for 1 kg of over forty products across eight supply-chain stages. I chose twelve that span the full range — from beef at 99.5 kg CO₂e to root vegetables at 0.43 kg — mixing animal and plant foods, staples and luxuries, so the contrast reads from across the room.

Before picking a form, I mapped the character of the data.

I listed words that describe these emissions and placed them on two axes: conceptual ↔ experiential, invisible ↔ physical. Almost everything clustered on the invisible side — the emissions are real, embedded and disproportionate, yet completely hidden from the person eating. That became the brief for the metaphor: make the hidden cost physical.

Four sketches before the kebab.

Each idea tried a different way to make an invisible number feel heavy in the hand. I sketched them on iPad, tested what each would say to a passer-by, and kept the one that was both familiar and impossible to ignore.

01

Stacked stage blocks

Every stage of the supply chain becomes a block — land-use change, farming, animal feed — stacked along a line for each food. Bigger block, more emissions.

02

Grocery-bag mobile

Hanging shopping bags, one per food, banded in stage colours. A link to the moment we actually choose food — at the shop.

03

Mushroom caps

Organic, mushroom-like forms where stage colours ring the cap. Playful, but the quantities were hard to read accurately.

04

The diet scale

A weighing scale comparing an average daily diet against a vegan diet — the total kg of greenhouse gas on each pan.

“Paneer kebab and the stick” — the note that started it

Idea 01’s stacked blocks, threaded on a stick, already looked like a kebab. A skewer is linear like a supply chain, its length can carry quantity, everyone recognises it — and a grill gave me smoke, the missing piece of the story.

“We don’t care how the kebab got to our plate. We just want the kebab.”

A kebab is a good source of protein — and almost everyone’s favourite. But as humans, we rarely think about what happened behind the plate: the land cleared, the animals fed, the trucks, the packaging, the food thrown away. We only want the kebab on our plate.

​

So I put that blind spot on a grill. As each skewer cooks, it gives off smoke — and that smoke is the greenhouse gas released at every step of the food’s supply chain. The tastier it looks, the easier it is to forget.

the smoke carries everything we don’t see

Two inches of kebab = ten kilos of CO₂e.

Every skewer holds 1 kg of a food. I converted each stage’s emissions into length at a fixed scale (× 0.2 inch per kg), then cut that length into 2-inch blocks plus one remainder block. Colour tells you the stage; length tells you how much.

1 block = 2 in = 10 kg

the handle: always 1 kg

Every grill skewer was built with exactly these lengths — measured from the spreadsheet, cut by hand.

From spreadsheet to steel, wire and paint.

Everything was built by hand in the NID workshop over a few weeks in September and October.

01

Cutting the skewers

Mild-steel rods were measured, clamped in the workshop vise and cut with a hacksaw — one rod per food, filed smooth at the tip.

02

Building the grill body

A long black tray became the grill, divided into chambers so the coal bed and lights could sit evenly under every skewer.

03

Weaving the grate

Thin wire was laid and tied into a grid to make the grate — the surface the skewers rest on, just like a street-side grill.

04

Shaping & painting the blocks

Every block was cut to its exact length from the spreadsheet, then hand-painted in its stage colour with a charred, just-grilled texture.

05

Turning the handles

Wooden handles carry the food’s name. Each one stands for the same thing — 1 kg of that food — so only the blocks change.

06

Threading the skewers

Blocks went on in a fixed order — land-use change, farm, feed, processing, transport, retail, packaging, losses — so every skewer reads the same way.

07

Firing up the grill

Coals, embers and warm lights went under the grate, a ruler along the edge, and finally each skewer was laid in its place, heaviest first.

Twelve skewers on a live grill.

Lit from below and smoking gently, the grill invites people to lean in the way they would at a street stall. Only then does the ruler along its edge reveal what the length of each skewer means.

the ruler turns length into kg

Beef’s skewer is almost 20 inches long.

Root vegetables barely leave the handle.

Two posters to read the grill by.

One answers the question behind the project and shows the full dataset; the other is a key — the anatomy of a skewer, the eight stage colours, and the scale of each block.

What I learned

01

A familiar object lowers the barrier.

People already know how to read a kebab. Borrowing that literacy meant the data needed almost no explanation before people started comparing.

02

Physical length makes ratios felt.

On screen, 99.5 vs 0.43 is just two numbers. On a grill, it is the difference between a full skewer and a bare handle.

03

Precision and craft pull against each other.

Every block had to match the spreadsheet, but the charred texture had to feel hand-made. Planning the lengths first made the craft possible.
